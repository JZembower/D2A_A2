\section{Shortest Path via Backward Induction}
\label{sec:q1}

The shortest path problem is a classic example in dynamic programming. It involves finding the most efficient route through a network where each step has an associated cost. Rather than solving the entire problem at once, dynamic programming breaks it into smaller stages, solving each subproblem and building up to the overall solution. This approach is especially useful when decisions at one stage affect future outcomes.

In this problem, you are asked to plan a trip from your home to one of the downtown parking lots. Nodes constitute the traffic network (Figure~\ref{fig:net}). The number in the \textbf{upper-left corner} of each node denotes its node ID and the number \textbf{inside the node} specifies the travel time required to pass through that node. \textbf{Your home is located at Node \texttt{A3}, while the parking lots are represented by any node in the rightmost column} (Nodes \texttt{F1}-\texttt{F6}). Each intermediate node corresponds to a neighborhood. The edges represent neighborhood boundaries, and crossing a boundary does not add any additional time. 

The objective is to determine the \textbf{shortest path from your home to a parking lot} (any one of \texttt{F1}-\texttt{F6}). Although one could use forward calculation by enumerating all possible paths from the home node to a parking lot, it is computationally expensive. A more efficient method is to apply Backward Induction (BI) to the network. 


\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{parking.png}
    \caption{Path Network}
    \label{fig:net}
\end{figure}


\newpage

The problem can be formulated as a dynamic decision process with multiple stages, where the six columns of neighborhoods represent the six stages, with the following notations:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Stage} $t\in\{1,2,\dots,6\}$ represents the column in the network, where $T=6$ is the terminal stage
    \item \textbf{State} $s_t$, with $s_T$ as the terminal state and 
    \item \textbf{Action} $a(s_t)$, e.g. $a(s_t=\texttt{A3})=\texttt{B3}$
    \item \textbf{Transition Set} $N(s_t)$ is a set of all feasible next states that can be reached from the current state $s_t$
    \item \textbf{Cost Function} $g(s_t)$, e.g. $g(s_t=\texttt{B3})=7$
    \item \textbf{Cost-to-go Value} $J_t(s_t)$, with $J_T(s_T)$ for the terminal stage
\end{itemize}

\item \grade{5} \saq Write the cost-to-go function \textit{(Tip: formualte $J_t(s_t)$ and $J_T(s_T)$ separately)}.
\begin{answerBox}[height=1.6in]
At the terminal stage $t=T=6$ nothing remains to be decided, so the cost-to-go of a parking lot is just its own travel time.
\[ J_T(s_T) \;=\; g(s_T), \qquad s_T \in \{\texttt{F1},\dots,\texttt{F6}\} \]
Every earlier stage is then computed backward for $t = T-1$ down to $t = 1$.
\[ J_t(s_t) \;=\; \min_{s_{t+1}\in N(s_t)}\bigl\{\, g(s_t) + J_{t+1}(s_{t+1}) \,\bigr\} \;=\; g(s_t) \;+ \min_{s_{t+1}\in N(s_t)} J_{t+1}(s_{t+1}) \]
Cost sits on the nodes and not on the edges, so $g(s_t)$ is paid whichever neighbor we pick and it factors out of the $\min$. The stored action is $a(s_t) = \arg\min_{s_{t+1}\in N(s_t)} J_{t+1}(s_{t+1})$.
\end{answerBox}


\item \grade{10} \sCal Compute the cost-to-go value for node \texttt{D4}, $J_4(s_4=\texttt{D4})$. 
\\Include the calculation steps from the terminal stage to stage 4.
What is the action $a(s_4=\texttt{D4})$?
\begin{answerBox}[height=6in]
\textbf{Stage 6.} Nothing remains to be decided once we reach a parking lot, so every terminal value is just that node's own travel time.
\[ J_6(\texttt{F1})=8,\quad J_6(\texttt{F2})=2,\quad J_6(\texttt{F3})=6,\quad J_6(\texttt{F4})=5,\quad J_6(\texttt{F5})=5,\quad J_6(\texttt{F6})=3 \]

\textbf{Stage 5.} From \texttt{D4} the only feasible next states are $N(\texttt{D4})=\{\texttt{E3},\texttt{E4}\}$, so those are the two values we actually need.
\begin{align*}
J_5(\texttt{E3}) &= g(\texttt{E3}) + \min\{J_6(\texttt{F3}),\, J_6(\texttt{F4})\} = 7 + \min\{6,\,5\} = 7 + 5 = 12 \\
J_5(\texttt{E4}) &= g(\texttt{E4}) + \min\{J_6(\texttt{F4}),\, J_6(\texttt{F5})\} = 9 + \min\{5,\,5\} = 9 + 5 = 14
\end{align*}
The two continuations from \texttt{E4} are tied at 5, so either one is optimal there.

\textbf{Stage 4.}
\begin{align*}
J_4(s_4=\texttt{D4}) &= g(\texttt{D4}) \;+ \min_{s_5 \in N(\texttt{D4})} J_5(s_5)
= 3 + \min\{\, J_5(\texttt{E3}),\, J_5(\texttt{E4}) \,\} \\
&= 3 + \min\{12,\, 14\} \;=\; 3 + 12 \;=\; \mathbf{15}
\end{align*}
The minimum is attained at \texttt{E3}, so $J_4(s_4=\texttt{D4}) = 15$ and $a(s_4=\texttt{D4}) = \texttt{E3}$. Only five of the 36 nodes were needed to answer this, which is the whole point of working backward instead of enumerating paths.
\end{answerBox}

\[ \texttt{A3} \;\rightarrow\; \texttt{B4} \;\rightarrow\; \texttt{C4} \;\rightarrow\; \texttt{D5} \;\rightarrow\; \texttt{E5} \;\rightarrow\; \texttt{F6} \]
The travel times add to $7+3+6+3+1+3 = 23$, which matches $J_1(\texttt{A3})$ as it should. Note that \texttt{F2} is the cheapest lot at 2 and we still do not end there, since reaching it costs more than the single unit it saves. \texttt{D4} is not on this path either, even though part (e) valued it, because backward induction values every state and not only the ones we end up visiting.
\end{answerBox}

\item \grade{2} \saq When calling the \texttt{step()} function of \texttt{HospitalEnvQL}, one of the results is variable of next state. What does this next state variable include? Explain in words what it/they means/mean in the context of hospital surge staffing.
\begin{answerBox}[height=2in]
It is a tuple of two integers, $(t_{\text{next}},\, x_{\text{next}})$, which are the next hour of the day and the number of patients still waiting at the start of that hour.

Inside \texttt{step()} the queue updates as \texttt{x\_next = np.clip(x + d - cap, 0, X\_MAX)}, with \texttt{d = rng.poisson(lam)} and \texttt{cap = 2*(10 + u)}. In simpler terms we take who is already waiting, add the patients who walked in this hour, subtract everyone the 10 base plus $u$ surge doctors can treat at 2 patients per hour, and do not go below zero.

The clock has to be part of the state and not just the queue. Twenty patients waiting at 9am and twenty at 7pm call for completely different decisions, since at 9am there are hours of capacity left to work the queue down while at 7pm every remaining patient triggers the terminal penalty $W = \$300$. That is why the notebook calls $(t,x)$ the augmented state. When $t+1 > T$ the variable becomes $(13, x_{\text{next}})$, which flags the end of the day so the terminal cost is charged.
\end{answerBox}

\paragraph{Q-table} is one of the keys in tabular Q-learning. In the notebook, \texttt{QLearningAgent} initializes the Q-table using the code below, which is a 3-dimensional table. 

\begin{lstlisting}
        self.Q = np.zeros((T+2, X_MAX+1, U_MAX+1), dtype=float)

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), resume]

\item \grade{2} \saq What does each dimension represent in the context of hospital surge staffing?
\begin{answerBox}[height=2in]
The table is indexed \texttt{Q[t, x, u]}, so the three dimensions are the stage, the state and the action.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Size \texttt{T+2} is the stage $t$, meaning the hour of the day for $t = 1,\dots,T = 12$, plus an unused index 0 so the hours stay 1-indexed, plus the terminal stage $t = T+1 = 13$.
\item Size \texttt{X\_MAX+1} is the state $x_t$, the number of patients waiting at the start of hour $t$, running from 0 to \texttt{X\_MAX}.
\item Size \texttt{U\_MAX+1} is the action $u_t$, the number of on-demand doctors staffed in hour $t$, running from 0 to \texttt{U\_MAX}.
\end{itemize}
Each entry is then the expected cumulative cost of staffing $u$ surge doctors when $x$ patients are waiting in hour $t$ and behaving optimally afterward.
\end{answerBox}


\item \grade{2} \saq Why is it \texttt{X\_MAX+1} instead of \texttt{X\_MAX} directly?
\begin{answerBox}[height=2in]
The queue length is used directly as a zero-based array index, and it can take any integer value from 0 to \texttt{X\_MAX} inclusive. That is $\texttt{X\_MAX}+1$ distinct states rather than \texttt{X\_MAX}.

Both endpoints are real states that have to be stored. An empty waiting room sits at index 0, and a full waiting room needs index \texttt{X\_MAX} to exist. Sizing the axis at \texttt{X\_MAX} would make $\texttt{X\_MAX}-1$ the largest valid index and raise an \texttt{IndexError} the first time \texttt{np.clip} pushes the queue up to its cap.

The same counting gives \texttt{U\_MAX+1}, since staffing no extra doctors is itself a valid action and it occupies index 0.
\end{answerBox}



\end{enumerate}

\item \grade{2} \saq The current $a_t$ follows a time-dependent linear decay until it reaches a minimum threshold. How would you modify this schedule to use a non-linear decay instead? \textbf{Express your answer as a formula of the form} $a_t = \dots$. I would replace the subtractive step with a multiplicative one, which gives geometric decay down to the same floor.
\[ \alpha_t \;=\; \max\bigl\{\, \alpha_{\min},\;\; \alpha_0 \cdot \rho^{\,t} \,\bigr\}, \qquad 0 < \rho < 1 \]
This is the same as $\alpha_t = \max\{\alpha_{\min},\, \alpha_0 e^{-\lambda t}\}$ with $\lambda = -\ln \rho$, and $\alpha_0 = 0.3$, $\rho = 0.9995$, $\alpha_{\min} = 0.05$ would be reasonable here. In code it is one character of change, since \texttt{self.lr - self.lr\_dec} becomes \texttt{self.lr * self.lr\_dec}. Removing a constant fraction each step rather than a constant amount means the rate drops quickly while the Q-values are still crude and then flattens into a long tail of fine tuning.

A time-independent alternative would decay each state and action pair by its own visit count $N_t(x_t,u_t)$.
\[ \alpha_t \;=\; \frac{\alpha_0}{1 + \kappa\, N_t(x_t, u_t)} \]
I would prefer this one for the hospital problem, since visits are very uneven and a global clock shrinks $\alpha$ for rarely seen queue lengths before they have learned anything. It also satisfies the Robbins-Monro conditions $\sum_t \alpha_t = \infty$ and $\sum_t \alpha_t^2 < \infty$ that the convergence proof needs.
\end{answerBox}



\end{enumerate}


\paragraph{SARSA} (State-Action-Reward-State-Action) is a simpler reinforcement learning algorithm than Q-learning. The agent works similarly to Q-learning that takes actions, receives feedback, and continuously updates its behavior to maximize long-term rewards. 

SARSA updates the Q-value using Equation~\ref{eq:sarsa}. 

\begin{equation}
    Q_t(x_t, u_t) \leftarrow (1-\alpha_t) Q_t(x_t,u_t) + \alpha_t \Bigl( r_t + \gamma Q_{t+1}(x_{t+1}, u_{t+1})\Bigr)
    \label{eq:sarsa}
\end{equation}

As you can see, the key difference is that SARSA does not include $\min$ operation. Therefore, SARSA is an \textbf{on-policy} algorithm learning the value of the policy it is actually following, while Q-learning is \textbf{off-policy} that learns the value of the optimal policy while possibly following a different exploratory policy when taking actions.


\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), resume]

\item \grade{3} \sAll Which of the algorithms eventually converge to the same Q-values, despite differences in the number of steps required for convergence?
{
\checkboxchar{$\Box$} \checkedchar{$\blacksquare$}  % DO NOT CHANGE THIS LINE

    \choice SARSA with the same $\epsilon$-greedy, $\epsilon = 0.1$
    \CorrectChoice SARSA with dynamic $\epsilon$-greedy, where $\epsilon$ is gradually reduced from 0.1 to 0
    \choice All three algorithms above lead to different Q-values
\end{checkboxes}
}


\item \grade{2} \saq Reason your answer to the above question.
\begin{answerBox}[height=4in]
The three differ in which policy the update target is evaluating, not in how fast they learn.

Q-learning with a fixed $\epsilon$ converges to $Q^*$. Its target bootstraps from $\min_{u_{t+1}} Q_{t+1}(x_{t+1}, u_{t+1})$, which is the value of the best next action rather than the action actually taken. That makes it off-policy and a sample-based version of the Bellman optimality equation, so behaving randomly 10\% of the time changes which cells get visited but not the fixed point they settle on.

SARSA with the same fixed $\epsilon$ does not. Its target bootstraps from $Q_{t+1}(x_{t+1}, u_{t+1})$ where $u_{t+1}$ is the action the $\epsilon$-greedy policy will really take, so it is on-policy and converges to $Q^\pi$ for that exploratory policy instead. Since the policy takes a random and often expensive action 10\% of the time, SARSA is correctly pricing in the cost of its own exploration, and its values stay pessimistic relative to $Q^*$. This is not a bug, it is a different question being answered.

SARSA with $\epsilon$ decayed to zero converges to $Q^*$ again. The policy being evaluated becomes the greedy policy in the limit, so $Q^\pi \to Q^*$ and the on-policy target lines up with the off-policy one.

So options 1 and 3 share the same limit while option 2 does not. The notebook shows this on the 1-D driving example, where the exact $Q^*(1,\text{R})$ is 6.20, Q-learning reaches 6.20, SARSA with a decaying $\epsilon$ reaches 6.18, and SARSA with a fixed $\epsilon$ only reaches about 5.75.
\end{answerBox}


\end{enumerate}

\begin{answerBox}[height=6in]
Both curves are what the two random forests predict for one fixed scenario as the surge multiplier is swept across its whole feasible range from 1 to 3. The time of day, day of week and weather score are held constant, so the only thing moving along the horizontal axis is the decision itself.

The supply curve rises. A larger multiplier pays drivers more, so more of them make themselves available, and predicted supply climbs from roughly 131 at $s=1$ to roughly 188 at $s=3$. The demand curve falls, since the same multiplier is a higher fare to riders, and predicted demand drops from roughly 141 down to roughly 72 over that range. Neither curve is smooth, because both are tree ensembles and they move in flat steps rather than as continuous functions.

Matches are $\min\{y_S,\, y_D\}$, so the objective is the lower envelope of the two curves and not either curve on its own. Left of the crossing point supply is the short side, so raising the multiplier recruits drivers and adds matches. Right of the crossing demand is the short side, so raising it further only prices riders out and destroys matches. The peak of the lower envelope therefore sits at the crossing, and that is where the black dashed line lands. In my run the optimum came out just below $s = 1.2$ with about 135 matches, a little to the left of where the curves meet near $s = 1.22$, and supply was still the binding side there.

Two things stand out to me. For one, the optimal multiplier is modest. Across all ten scenarios it only ranges from about 1.12 to 1.35 and never approaches the cap of 3, so aggressive surge is counterproductive when the goal is matching rather than revenue. The other point is that this plot is a picture of why the problem is decision dependent. The multiplier is both the decision variable and an input feature to both predictors, so the forecast moves as the decision moves. We cannot predict once and then optimize against a fixed number, which is exactly why the fitted forests are embedded inside the Gurobi model rather than being called before it.
